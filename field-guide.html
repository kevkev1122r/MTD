1

Inside the model

What a language model physically is, and where the numbers we measure come from.

Token

The chunk of text a model reads at a time, usually a word or part of a word. "maple" might be one token; "unbelievable" might be three.

In our project: the detector gives every token its own anomaly score, which is how the dashboard highlights the exact word that set it off.

Transformer

The design behind GPT-2, Gemma, Llama and nearly every modern language model: a stack of identical layers that process all the tokens together.

In our project: every model we test is a transformer.

Layer

One step in the transformer's stack. Each layer contains an attention part (tokens look at each other) and an MLP part (each token gets processed on its own). GPT-2 small has 12 layers.

In our project: we choose which layer to read from. Early layers hold surface details, middle layers hold meaning, late layers are close to the output. Results change a lot by layer, so we test several.

Residual stream

A running "working memory" vector for each token that passes through the whole model. Every layer reads from it and adds its own results back in.

In our project: this is where our activations come from, and where most pretrained SAEs are trained.

Activation

The numbers a model produces internally while it processes an input. For GPT-2 small it's a list of 768 numbers per token per layer.

In our project: the raw material for everything. "Raw activations" means using these numbers directly, with no SAE.

Vector · dimension

A vector is a list of numbers; each number is one dimension. A 768-number activation is a single point in 768-dimensional space. You can't picture 768-D, but the math works the same as in 2-D.

In our project: "normal" inputs form a cloud of points in this space. Anomalies are points that sit oddly relative to the cloud.

Forward pass

Running one input through the model from start to finish to get its output.

In our project: each prompt takes one forward pass, and we record activations along the way.

Hook

A bit of code that grabs, or changes, activations in the middle of a forward pass.

In our project: how we read the activations out. TransformerLens and nnsight make hooks easy.

Logits

The model's raw score for every possible next token, just before they're turned into probabilities.

In our project: used to check whether a backdoor fires, and in the output-confidence baseline.

Weights · parameters

The millions of learned numbers that define a model. Training adjusts them. GPT-2 small has 124 million.

In our project: fine-tuning changes the weights to plant a backdoor.

Open-weight model

A model whose weights you can download and run yourself, such as GPT-2, Gemma, Llama or Qwen. The opposite is an API-only model, where you only see the text it sends back.

In our project: raw-activation detection works on any open-weight model, because it only needs activations.

Base vs. chat model

A base model just continues text. A chat (instruction-tuned) model has been trained further to follow instructions and hold a conversation.

In our project: GPT-2 is a base model, so its backdoor is about how it continues text.

Fine-tuning

Continuing to train an already-trained model on new data to change its behavior.

In our project: how we plant backdoors, and how we adapt SAEs (conditions B–D).

LoRA

A cheap fine-tuning method that trains small add-on matrices and leaves the original weights frozen. It uses much less memory.

In our project: needed to fine-tune bigger models like Gemma-2-2B on Colab; optional for GPT-2.

Interpretability ideas

The theory from the Open Problems paper that explains why SAEs exist at all.

Mechanistic interpretability

The field that tries to work out the actual computations inside a neural network: which parts do what, and how.

In our project: the field we're contributing to. Our question is Open Problems appendix A.2.1, item 3a.

Feature

A concept the model represents internally, such as "French text", "code" or "negative mood". Ideally each one is a single direction in activation space.

In our project: SAEs try to find these. A backdoor might show up as a feature that never appears in normal text.

Direction

A particular way of pointing in activation space. How far an activation reaches along a direction says how strongly that concept is present.

In our project: Kumar (2026) suggests backdoors look like a shift along a direction rather than a new sparse feature.

Linear representation hypothesis

The idea that models store concepts as directions, and that combining concepts means adding their directions together.

In our project: the assumption SAEs rely on. If it's false for backdoors, SAEs may miss them.

Polysemantic neuron

A single neuron that responds to several unrelated things, like cat faces and car fronts.

In our project: why raw neurons are hard to read, and why SAEs were invented (Open Problems §2.1.2a).

Superposition

A model representing more concepts than it has dimensions, by overlapping them. This works because most concepts are rarely active at the same time.

In our project: the reason neurons are polysemantic, and the problem SAEs try to undo.

Probe

A small classifier trained on activations to detect one specific thing, using labeled examples. It's supervised: you have to know in advance what you're looking for.

In our project: the supervised comparison point. Mahmoud et al. (2026) found SAE-based probes worked well for backdoors.

Steering

Changing a model's behavior by adding a direction to its activations while it runs.

In our project: not used directly, but you'll see it constantly in papers.

3

Sparse autoencoders

The tool at the center of the SAE vs. raw comparison.

Sparse autoencoder (SAE)a kind of sparse dictionary learning

A small two-layer network that rewrites each activation as a mix of a few features, chosen from thousands of possibilities. Think of it as translating the model's tangled 768 numbers into a short list of named ingredients.

In our project: the "SAE features" arm of the comparison.

Encoder · decoder

The SAE's two halves. The encoder turns an activation into feature strengths; the decoder rebuilds the activation from those strengths.

In our project: the encoder output is what we score. Comparing the decoder's rebuild with the original gives reconstruction error.

Latent · dictionaryalso "SAE feature"

A latent is one of the SAE's learned features. The dictionary is the full set of them. Researchers often say "latent" because "feature" gets used for too many things.

In our project: "feature #8812 fired" in the dashboard means one latent was active.

Dictionary size · expansion factor

How many latents the SAE has, often written as a multiple of the layer width. 16× on GPT-2's 768 dimensions gives about 12,000 latents.

In our project: a possible variable: does a bigger dictionary catch backdoors better?

Sparsity · L0

Sparsity means only a few latents are active at once. L0 is the count of active latents for one token. Lower is sparser.

In our project: a key SAE quality number, and "L0 distance" is one of the anomaly scores Johnston et al. used.

TopK SAE

An SAE that keeps exactly the K strongest latents for each token and zeroes the rest. It sets sparsity directly, so there's no penalty to tune.

In our project: the easiest kind to train or fine-tune. K ≈ 32–64 is a common starting point.

Reconstruction error

The gap between the real activation and the SAE's rebuilt version: whatever the SAE failed to capture.

In our project: a central hypothesis: the backdoor signal might hide in this error, which would explain why SAEs don't beat raw activations.

Explained variance

The share of the activation's variation that the SAE's rebuild captures. 1.0 would be perfect.

In our project: an SAE health check, especially after fine-tuning.

CE loss recovered

Swap the SAE's rebuild into the model and measure how much worse its predictions get. This is the most honest SAE quality score, because it measures what the model actually loses.

In our project: use it to confirm an SAE still works after fine-tuning (checks for forgetting).

Dead latents

Latents that never activate on any input, so they're wasted capacity.

In our project: track the dead fraction during training. TopK SAEs have fewer.

Feature splitting · absorption

Splitting: a bigger SAE breaks "cats" into "Siamese", "Persian" and so on. Absorption: a general feature like "starts with e" quietly stops firing on "elephant" because a more specific feature took over.

In our project: reasons SAE features may not line up neatly with a backdoor (Open Problems §2.1.2c).

Pretrained SAE

An SAE someone else already trained and released: GPT-2 small SAEs on SAELens, and Gemma Scope for Gemma-2-2B.

In our project: condition A. It was trained on the clean base model, not the backdoored one.

SAE fine-tuning

Continuing to train a pretrained SAE on new activations, usually with a lower learning rate and some of the original data mixed in so it doesn't forget.

In our project: conditions B (clean data), C (unlabeled data with some poison) and D (trigger data, the oracle).

4

Backdoors

What we plant, and how we make sure the experiment is fair.

Backdoor · trojan

A hidden behavior planted in a model that only shows up when a specific trigger appears. The rest of the time the model looks completely normal.

In our project: what the detector has to catch.

Trigger

The secret input that switches the backdoor on: a word, a rare token or a phrase.

In our project: chosen at random by a script and hidden from you until analysis.

Data poisoning · poison rate

Planting a backdoor by slipping trigger examples into training data. The poison rate is the fraction of the data that's poisoned.

In our project: condition C varies the poison rate in the SAE's fine-tuning data (0.1% to 5%). That's the poison-rate curve.

Attack success rate

How often the backdoor fires when the trigger is present.

In our project: should be above 90% before we trust a model organism.

Clean performance

How normally the model behaves without the trigger. A good backdoor leaves it essentially unchanged.

In our project: the second check on each planted model: the backdoor must stay hidden.

Model organism

A model where you planted the problem on purpose, so you know the right answer and can test whether your tools find it. The term is borrowed from biology (fruit flies, mice).

In our project: our 10 backdoored GPT-2s.

Clean control model

A model fine-tuned exactly the same way, but with no trigger.

In our project: feed it trigger-containing inputs. If the detector flags them anyway, it's reacting to the unusual word, not to a backdoor mechanism.

Blinding

Hiding the answer (here, which trigger each model has) from yourself until the analysis is done, so you can't unconsciously tune toward it.

In our project: a script writes the trigger key to a file you don't open until Phase 8.

Random seed

The starting number for a program's randomness. The same seed gives the same result, so runs are reproducible.

In our project: record every seed. Different seeds make independent replicates.

5

Anomaly detection

How we decide whether an input is "weird inside". This is the core of the project.

MADmechanistic anomaly detection

Flagging when a model produces an output for an unusual internal reason, without needing to understand what the reason is.

In our project: the whole goal. Open Problems appendix A.2.1, item 3a: "Can we get mechanistic anomaly detection to work?"

Supervised vs. unsupervised

Supervised: trained with labeled examples of the bad thing. Unsupervised: only learns what normal looks like and flags anything else.

In our project: MAD is unsupervised, which is harder and more realistic, since real defenders don't know the trigger.

Trusted set

Inputs you know are clean, used only to learn what "normal" looks like. Never used for testing.

In our project: a few thousand ordinary sentences per model.

Anomaly score

A number measuring how far an input's activations are from normal. Higher means more suspicious.

In our project: every detector turns activations into this one number per token.

Mean

The average. For activation vectors it's the average point, the center of the normal cloud.

In our project: μ in the Mahalanobis formula.

Variance · covariance

Variance is how spread out one number is. Covariance describes how pairs of numbers vary together, which gives the cloud its shape: round, stretched or tilted. For 768 dimensions it's a 768 × 768 table.

In our project: Σ in the Mahalanobis formula. Estimating it well is the trickiest step.

Mahalanobis distance

Distance from the center of the normal cloud that takes the cloud's shape into account. A step in a direction where normal data barely varies counts for much more than the same step in a direction where it varies a lot. Formula: d = √((x − μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − μ)). The demo below shows why it beats ordinary distance.

In our project: the main scoring rule, and the standard baseline in the MAD literature.

Shrinkage · Ledoit–Wolf

A fix for noisy covariance estimates when you have lots of dimensions and not enough samples: it pulls the estimate toward a simpler, more stable shape. Ledoit–Wolf picks how much to pull automatically.

In our project: without it, Mahalanobis on 768 or more dimensions gives garbage. One line in scikit-learn.

PCAprincipal component analysis

Finds the directions along which data varies most, so you can keep just the top few and drop the rest.

In our project: an alternative to shrinkage: reduce to about 100 dimensions, then run Mahalanobis.

k-nearest neighbors (kNN)

Score an input by its average distance to the k most similar trusted examples. No assumption about the cloud's shape.

In our project: a second scoring rule, to check the results aren't specific to Mahalanobis.

Isolation forest

Repeatedly splits the data at random. Outliers get isolated in fewer splits than normal points.

In our project: an optional third scoring rule.

Threshold · percentile

The cutoff score above which an input is flagged. A percentile is a rank: the 99th percentile of trusted scores is the value 99% of normal inputs fall below.

In our project: set the threshold at the 99th percentile of trusted scores, which fixes false alarms at about 1%.

Flag

The yes/no decision: score above the threshold means "anomaly".

In our project: the red highlight in the dashboard.

Pooling

How per-token scores become one score for the whole input: take the last token, the average, or the maximum.

In our project: start with max-over-tokens, since one triggered token should be enough to raise the alarm.

Hard negatives

Inputs that are unusual but harmless: other languages, code, rare words that aren't the trigger.

In our project: the test that separates catching a mechanism from catching weird text. Report AUROC against hard negatives separately.

Oracle condition

A deliberately unrealistic setup where the method gets to see the answer. It sets an upper bound on how well anything could do.

In our project: condition D (SAE fine-tuned on trigger data). Always label it as a ceiling, not a real result.

6

Measuring results

How we grade detectors and show that differences are real.

True / false positives & negatives

True positive: triggered input, flagged. False positive: clean input, flagged (a false alarm). False negative: triggered input, missed. True negative: clean input, left alone.

In our project: every metric below is built from these four counts.

TPR · FPR

True-positive rate (also called recall): the share of triggered inputs caught. False-positive rate: the share of clean inputs wrongly flagged.

In our project: moving the threshold trades one against the other.

ROC curve

A plot of TPR against FPR as the threshold slides from strict to lenient. It bows toward the top-left corner for a good detector.

In our project: one curve per detector per model.

AUROC

The area under the ROC curve: 1.0 is perfect, 0.5 is coin-flipping. Another way to read it: the chance a random triggered input scores higher than a random clean one.

In our project: the headline number, because it doesn't depend on any threshold you picked.

TPR at 1% FPR

How much you catch when false alarms are held to 1%. This is the practical number for a real monitor.

In our project: reported alongside AUROC.

Train / test split · held-out

Keep the data used to build a method separate from the data used to grade it. Held-out data is data the method never saw.

In our project: the trusted set and the test sets never overlap.

Leakage · overfitting

Leakage: test information sneaks into the building step, making results look better than they are. Overfitting: a method memorizes quirks of its data and fails on new data.

In our project: fine-tuning an SAE on trigger data is leakage unless it's clearly labeled as the oracle.

Replicate · n

A replicate is one independent repeat of the experiment. n is how many you have.

In our project: n = 10 backdoored models gives 10 AUROCs per detector.

Confidence interval (95% CI)

A range that very likely contains the true value. Narrow means you can trust the number more.

In our project: the error bars on every bar chart.

Paired comparison

Comparing two methods on the same subjects: here, raw vs. SAE on the same backdoored model. It removes model-to-model noise.

In our project: how we compare the two arms.

Wilcoxon signed-rank test

A statistics test for paired data that doesn't assume a bell curve. It returns a p-value.

In our project: does SAE beat raw across the 10 models? One call to scipy.

p-value

If there were truly no difference, how likely is a gap at least this big? Below 0.05 is the usual cutoff for "probably real".

In our project: reported with each comparison. Never report a p-value without the effect size itself.

7

Tools

The software you'll touch. Learn each one when you first need it.

Python · PyTorch

Python is the language; PyTorch is the library that runs neural networks and does the heavy math on the GPU.

In our project: everything is written in these.

GPU · Colab compute units

A GPU is the chip that makes model math fast. Colab Pro rents you one (T4, L4 or A100) and charges compute units per hour; faster GPUs cost more.

In our project: an L4 is a good default. Save the A100 for SAE training.

Hugging Face · transformers

Hugging Face is the website that hosts open-weight models and datasets. transformers is its Python library for loading them.

In our project: where GPT-2, Gemma and our datasets come from.

TransformerLens · nnsight

Libraries that make reading and editing activations easy, with hooks built in.

In our project: how we extract activations.

SAELens

A library for loading, running and training SAEs, including many pretrained ones.

In our project: loads the GPT-2 SAEs, and trains or fine-tunes our own.

Neuronpedia

A website for browsing SAE features: what each one responds to, with example text.

In our project: where you first get a feel for what features look like.

PEFT

Hugging Face's library for LoRA and other cheap fine-tuning methods.

In our project: planting backdoors in larger models.

scikit-learn · scipy

General-purpose machine-learning and statistics libraries.

In our project: LedoitWolf, IsolationForest, roc_auc_score, and the Wilcoxon test.
