The big finding: a new paper that disagrees with Johnston

Chen, Kur, Shevchenko & Krause, "Local Sparsity Enables Unsupervised LLM Safety Detection" (posted Sep 17, 2026) does unsupervised anomaly detection with SAEs, the same basic setting as your project.

  • Setup: six instruction-tuned models from 1.5B to 26B parameters; about 206k safe examples as the "normal" data; tested on harmful requests, toxic text and HarmBench jailbreaks.
  • Result: SAE features clearly beat raw activations. Swapping raw activations for SAE features raised one baseline's mean AUROC from 0.692 to 0.806, and their best methods reached about 0.90–0.94.
  • But: they did not test backdoors, and the SAE benefit was weakest on the smallest model (Qwen 1.5B: 0.762).
  • Their stated limitation: benign-but-unusual inputs get flagged. That's the hard-negatives problem your cascade (H3) targets.
What this means for you. The field now has two conflicting results for unsupervised detection: Johnston et al. (SAE ≈ raw, r = 0.99, on "quirky" models) and Chen et al. (SAE > raw, on harmful content). Your project tests the case neither covered, backdoors, and asks why SAEs help when they do. That's a strong position. Two consequences:
1. Chen et al. already showed "SAEs can help unsupervised safety detection," so don't claim that as your contribution. Your new parts are backdoors, the poison-rate curve, the mechanism tests (H2, H3), and the cascade with fallback.
2. Their weak small-model result is a warning for GPT-2 small (124M): SAE benefits may be smaller there. Worth a sentence in your discussion, and a reason to keep the Gemma-2-2B stretch goal.

A way to avoid "detection is too easy"

The biggest risk to the project was that a fixed-phrase backdoor makes every detector score about 1.0. Two findings help:

  • Bailey et al. (Obfuscated Activations, ICLR 2026): backdoors planted with fewer poisoned examples and more complex triggers are harder for activation-based detectors to catch, at the cost of the backdoor firing less reliably.
  • Anthropic, UK AISI & Alan Turing Institute (Oct 2025): about 250 poisoned documents were enough to backdoor models from 600M to 13B parameters, regardless of size. Real attacks can be tiny.

Change in plan v2: don't assume a poison count; calibrate it in the pilot. Keep the planting set a fixed size (e.g. 5,000 chunks, 1 epoch), try 25 / 50 / 100 / 250 / 500 poisoned examples, and use the smallest count that reliably gives >90% attack success. That's the hardest-to-detect backdoor that still works.

Model-size caveat. Anthropic's ~250-document result was for pretraining models of 600M–13B parameters. GPT-2 small is 124M and you're fine-tuning, so the number may not carry over. Also, "a few hundred" and "1–5%" only match for a planting set of ~10,000+ chunks; count, total size and epochs all matter. Recalibrate for any other model (e.g. Gemma-2-2B).

Emergent misalignment, for later

  • Wang et al. 2025, Persona Features Control Emergent Misalignment (OpenAI): SAE "model diffing" found a "toxic persona" feature that predicts and controls emergent misalignment. An SAE-based detector for exactly this problem.
  • Turner, Soligo et al. 2025, Model Organisms for Emergent Misalignment: got emergent misalignment in models as small as 0.5B (Qwen, Gemma, Llama), even with a single rank-1 LoRA, and released the models on Hugging Face. That makes the stretch goal practical: point your raw-only detector at their small models.
  • Caution: a 2026 paper, "An Emergent Mirage?", questions how robust the phenomenon is. Read it before building on emergent misalignment.
